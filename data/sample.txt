# Introduction to Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a technique that enhances Large Language
Models (LLMs) by giving them access to external knowledge at query time — without
retraining the model.

## Why RAG?

Standard LLMs are trained on a fixed dataset and have a knowledge cutoff. They
cannot know about:

- Your private company documents
- Recently published research papers
- Your personal notes or custom knowledge base
- Any information that postdates their training cutoff

RAG solves this by retrieving relevant passages from your documents and including
them in the LLM's prompt. The model then generates answers grounded in YOUR data.

## How RAG Works

The RAG pipeline has two phases:

### Phase 1: Ingestion

Before users can ask questions, documents must be indexed:

1. **Load** the document (PDF, text file, web page, etc.)
2. **Chunk** it into smaller passages (typically 200–1000 characters each)
3. **Embed** each chunk — convert it to a vector using an embedding model
4. **Store** the vectors and raw text in a vector database

This happens offline, before any queries arrive.

### Phase 2: Query

When a user asks a question:

1. **Embed** the question using the same embedding model
2. **Search** the vector database for chunks similar to the question
3. **Retrieve** the top-K most relevant chunks
4. **Prompt** the LLM with: "Given these passages: [...] Answer: {question}"
5. **Return** the LLM's answer (plus the source passages for transparency)

## Why Embeddings?

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that captures the *meaning* of text.

For example, these two sentences have very different words but similar embeddings:

  "The dog sprinted after the ball."
  "A puppy chased a toy."

Because embeddings capture semantics, we can find relevant passages even when
the user's question doesn't share exact keywords with the document.

## What Is a Vector Database?

A vector database stores embeddings and allows efficient similarity search —
finding the vectors closest to a query vector. Unlike SQL queries that match
exact values, vector search finds semantic neighbors.

Common choices: ChromaDB (local), Pinecone (cloud), Weaviate, Qdrant, pgvector.

This template uses ChromaDB — it's free, local, and requires zero configuration.

## The Grounding Principle

The key RAG constraint: the LLM is instructed to answer ONLY from the retrieved
passages, not from its general training knowledge. This prevents hallucination
and makes the system's answers traceable back to your actual documents.

## Key Parameters

**Chunk Size**
How many characters per chunk. Smaller chunks → more precise retrieval but
less context. Larger chunks → more context but less precise matching.
Rule of thumb: 300–800 characters works well for most prose.

**Chunk Overlap**
How many characters adjacent chunks share. Prevents information loss at
chunk boundaries. Typical overlap: 10–15% of chunk size.

**Top-K**
How many chunks to retrieve per query. More chunks = more context for the LLM,
but also more noise and a longer prompt. Typical values: 3–6.

## Limitations of RAG

- Quality of retrieval depends heavily on the embedding model
- Very long documents may require hierarchical chunking strategies
- RAG cannot answer questions that require reasoning across many passages
- The LLM may still occasionally ignore the retrieved context
